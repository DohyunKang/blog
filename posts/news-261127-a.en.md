The model name I kept seeing on X and YouTube all week was Inkling 2 Mini. It's a small model Thinking Machines Lab released with open weights on November 20, a younger sibling of the [Inkling 2 Small](#/post/news-261106-a) they put out earlier this month. I read the official announcement and the Hugging Face model card. I didn't download or run it. All benchmark numbers are the company's own.

## What's different
On paper it's a common small MoE model. MoE (Mixture of Experts) means the model holds many small networks called experts and only picks a few for each token. Of 26.4B total parameters, 3.4B are active per token, 8 of 128 experts. It takes text and images, outputs text, and has a context window of up to 1M tokens. The checkpoint is about 20GB, so it's said to run on a Mac with 32GB+ memory or a GPU with similar VRAM. Apache 2.0.

The difference is one phrase in the "model type" field: looped transformer. A looped transformer runs the same layers more than once for each token. The company says this is the first one it has released.

## The middle layers run three times
There are 24 layers in four blocks of six. The first and last blocks run once. The two middle blocks (12 layers) run three times with the same weights. So 24 stored layers become 48 layer passes per token.

Why the middle: the intuition is that the first and last layers mostly translate between tokens and internal representations, and the abstract thinking happens in between. In their tests, looping the middle 12 layers beat looping the last 12 on 27 of 28 evaluations.

Each pass doesn't just overwrite the last one. The middle blocks' output from the previous pass is called the "message," and the next pass starts from the first block's output mixed with that message. A learned gate sets the mix. Early in training it blocks the message completely, then opens up over time, so the model starts out like a normal non-looped one and training stays stable. That was my favorite part. They changed the architecture and planned pretty carefully not to break training while doing it.

It isn't free. Going through the middle three times means more compute per token than 3.4B active suggests. By the company's count it's about 5.4B. The limitations section says as much: don't estimate compute from active parameters alone.

## How they made it small
It didn't start small. Until midtraining it had 384 experts. At one point they cut the least-used two-thirds, leaving 128. Then they trained on 1T tokens distilled from a larger internal Inkling model so the remaining experts could take over the deleted ones' work. Distillation is having a small model learn by imitating a bigger model's answers. They call this pruning and healing.

Most weights were trained in NVFP4 (4-bit) from the start, with the last four layers in MXFP8 (8-bit). It ships in the same format, so there's no quantization loss from converting.

## Solving with less written thinking
The company's pitch for looping: running the same layers again gives the model more internal computation before it emits a token, so it reaches the same answer with fewer thinking tokens. In one example from the post, at 1 pass the model made mistakes and backtracked, using more than twice the reasoning tokens it needed at 5 passes.

Then the company raises its own concern. Right now we oversee models by reading the chain of thought (CoT) they write out. If more computation happens silently, more reasoning might skip the page. So they also released a research model where the loop count can be set from 1 to 5 (TML-d24-varloop-research) and tested it. With no CoT allowed, the length of task it could handle (in human time) went from 16 seconds at 1 pass to 37 seconds at 3. Beyond that it was mostly flat. On a task that chains lookups through several tables, though, it kept improving up to 10 loops, more than it was ever trained with. The CoT stayed readable, and at this size they didn't see monitoring get meaningfully harder. Whether anyone has independently confirmed this, I couldn't check.

They also write down what looping can't do. Thinking gets deeper but knowledge doesn't grow. Facts mostly depend on total parameter count, so closed-book QA scores barely moved with more loops. They say it covers the gap by using search and tools well.

## Why it's everywhere
By the official table it scores 48.2% on DeepSWE v1.1 and 79.2% on SWE-bench Verified, ahead of every open model under 100B on agentic coding benchmarks. It's close to Qwen3.8-27B on many rows. Add "runs on a laptop" on top. The announcement even has a demo of researchers building a flight simulator on a MacBook Pro in airplane mode.

YouTube has several new videos a day along the lines of "I ran it on my GPU" or "coding battle vs Qwen 3.8 27B." Some run it on 12GB or 16GB VRAM, but those are quantized builds, not the official recommended setup. Reddit reactions: couldn't check.

The limitations section is pretty honest. It says the model can get stuck re-checking the same thing while debugging code, and that with tools it may delete or overwrite files without asking, so run it in a sandbox. Quality may drop outside English. Korean isn't mentioned, so I couldn't check that.

## As a beginner
The usual way to make a small model smart has been to train big and shrink. This one gets depth by reusing the same layers. Less memory, a bit more compute. I also learned that picking a local model by "how many B" can mislead you on speed.

From a PM angle, the limitations list works as a checklist as is: cap output tokens and agent turns, and require confirmation before anything destructive. The smaller the model, the more those fences should be the default.
